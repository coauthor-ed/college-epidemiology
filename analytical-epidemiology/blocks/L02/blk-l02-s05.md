## The controls must be like the cases in every other way

The answer is that the link may be hidden. Bladder cancer is itself linked to smoking, so the controls were chosen from a group that already smokes more than ordinary men. **Controls must be free of the disease under study and as like the cases as possible in everything else.** The only difference allowed between the two groups is the disease itself.

<!-- STEP -->
## Four sources of controls, each with a weakness

Controls can come from four places. Each one brings its own risk.

- **Hospital patients** are easy to reach, but their own illnesses may share the exposure.
- **Relatives** are willing, but they share the cases' genes.
- **Neighbours** share the cases' streets, water and air.
- **The general population**, a random sample from a defined area, is the cleanest source and the slowest to assemble.

<!-- STEP -->
## Watch the controls change and the gap close

*Illustrative numbers.* Take a study of smoking and heart attack in which 60 of 100 cases smoke. Drawn from the general population, 40 of 100 controls smoke, and the two groups differ by 20 percentage points. Draw the controls from men with bladder cancer instead and 60 of 100 of them smoke. Nothing about the cases has changed, yet the gap has closed to nothing, and a real link has disappeared from the study.

<!-- STEP -->
## How many controls, and how many sources

One control for each case is enough when cases are plentiful. When the cases are few, as with a rare tumour, take two, three or even four controls for each case: the larger comparison group steadies a small study, though the gain after four is small. Controls from more than one source guard against the bias that any single source brings.

The source is only the first decision. The two groups must also be alike in things like age and sex, and that step is called matching.
