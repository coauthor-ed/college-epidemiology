## The controls must be like the cases in every other way

The answer was that the link may be hidden, because the controls already had a disease that is itself linked to smoking. **Controls must be free of the disease under study and as like the cases as possible in everything else.** The only difference allowed between the two groups is the disease itself.

## Select each source and read what can go wrong

Four sources, each with a weakness:
- **Hospital patients**: other illnesses share the exposure.
- **Relatives**: siblings share genes.
- **Neighbours**: same water and air.
- **General population**: a random sample, hardest to assemble.

## Watch the comparison change when the controls change

*Illustrative numbers.*

Take a study of smoking and heart attack. Sixty of 100 cases smoke. If the controls come from the general population, 40 of 100 of them smoke, and the two groups differ clearly. Switch the controls to hospital patients with bladder cancer and 60 of 100 of them smoke, because bladder cancer is linked to smoking too. Nothing about the cases has changed, but the gap between the two groups has closed. Select a source to switch.

## How many controls, and how many sources

One control for each case is enough when cases are plentiful. When the cases are few, as with a rare tumour in people under 50, take two, three or even four controls for each case, so that a small group is steadied by a larger comparison. Controls from more than one source guard against the bias that any single source brings.

The source is only the first decision. The two groups must also be alike in age and sex, and that step is called matching.
