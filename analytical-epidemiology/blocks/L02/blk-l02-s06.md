## Drinking looks like a cause of oesophageal cancer

*Illustrative numbers.* Each dot in the picture is one person. A blue dot has oesophageal cancer (a case), and a grey dot does not (a control). A filled dot drinks alcohol, a hollow dot does not, and a small spot on the corner of a dot means that person smokes.

In this case-control study, 60 of the 100 cases drink, but only 40 of the 112 controls drink. Sixty per cent against 36 per cent looks like a real difference, and it is easy to stop here and decide that alcohol causes this cancer.

<!-- STEP -->
## Split by smoking and the difference disappears

Now count the smokers: 55 of the 100 cases smoke, but only 22 of the 112 controls. Smoking is itself a cause of oesophageal cancer, and people who drink also smoke more often. So split each group into smokers and non-smokers, and compare drinking inside each part.

- Among smokers, 50 of 55 cases drink and 20 of 22 controls drink: 91 per cent in both.
- Among non-smokers, 10 of 45 cases drink and 20 of 90 controls drink: 22 per cent in both.

**The difference came from smoking, which goes together with drinking, not from alcohol itself.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Give the problem its name, and match it away

A factor like smoking here is a **confounding factor**, or confounder. It is linked to the exposure (drinking), it is itself a cause of the disease, and it is more common in one group than in the other.

**Matching** deals with it when the controls are chosen: for each case, we pick a control who has the same smoking habit. Then 55 of 100 controls smoke, as the cases do, and 60 of 100 drink in both groups. Smoking can no longer explain a difference in drinking, and no difference is left.

Age can be a confounder too: in studies of the contraceptive pill and breast cancer, the women taking the pill were younger. In **group matching**, each smoking group or age group makes up the same share of the cases and of the controls. In **pair matching**, each case gets its own control of the same age and sex.

<!-- STEP -->
## Never match on the thing you are testing

Suppose that, instead, for each case we pick a control who drinks the same way. Then cases and controls drink exactly alike, 60 of 100 each, because we chose them that way. Even if alcohol did cause this cancer, the study would still show 60 against 60, so it can no longer tell whether drinking matters.

A related mistake is **overmatching**: matching on too many things, or on something that drinking itself changes. Suitable controls then become hard to find, and a real difference is pulled down towards nothing.

**Match on things like smoking that could confuse the comparison, never on the thing you are testing.** The next file is a study that matched its controls well, and it found its cause.
