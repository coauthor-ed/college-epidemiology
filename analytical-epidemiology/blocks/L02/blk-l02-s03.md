## Fix what counts as a case before you look

Back to the live case and its 20 men with a rare liver tumour. Before a single patient is let in, you write down what counts as a case: the diagnostic criteria (here, a tumour confirmed by histology) and the stage. The definition is not changed while the study runs. If it drifts halfway through, you can no longer tell a real difference from a change in who was allowed in.

## Take newly diagnosed cases, not old ones

Over twelve years a clinic sees many patients diagnosed, but some die soon after diagnosis and never come back. The patients still attending today are the ones who survived long enough to be found, and survival can itself depend on the exposure. Take the patients newly diagnosed within a fixed period, here the last five years, and the series comes much closer to everyone who developed the disease.

## Where the cases come from decides who is missing

One hospital's series misses every patient who went somewhere else; a population registry reaches everyone in a defined area, but a registry may not exist for a rare tumour. Taking cases from two city hospitals, as this study does, comes closer to the whole city than one surgeon's list. Whatever source you use, state it plainly, because the controls must come from the same place.

**Cases from one place and controls from another can differ for reasons that have nothing to do with exposure.** That is the problem the next step has to solve.
