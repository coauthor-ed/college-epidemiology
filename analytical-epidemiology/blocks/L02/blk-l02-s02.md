## Start from the disease, look back for the exposure

The design that starts from people who are already ill is a **case-control study**. It studies two groups together: the **cases**, who have the disease, and the **controls**, who do not. The dots on the screen are people, drawn to show the order of events rather than a rate.

<!-- STEP -->
## The two groups are chosen, not counted

*Illustrative numbers: a school picnic, not the live case.*

At a school picnic, 20 people who ate there fell ill and 60 who ate there stayed well. The 20 ill people are the cases, and 60 well people are picked as controls so that every case has three people to compare with. Nobody counted how many people in the town fell ill, so the size of each group is the investigator's choice.

**The number of people in a case-control study tells you how many were studied, never how common the disease is.**

## Ask both groups the same question about the past

Here the exposure is the dish: who ate the paneer salad. Both groups are asked the same question, and the dots sort into four cells: ill and exposed, ill and unexposed, well and exposed, well and unexposed.

## The letters come last

The cells are named a, b, c and d: exposed cases, exposed controls, unexposed cases and unexposed controls. The margins are the group totals, a+c for the cases and b+d for the controls. Select any cell or margin under the table to light the people it holds. These letters are short names for counts you can already see, so read them after the picture, never before it.

## Three features and four steps

Three things define the design:
- exposure and disease have both already happened;
- the inquiry runs back from effect to cause;
- a comparison group is required.

Any case-control study then takes four steps: choose the cases, choose the controls, measure exposure in both groups in the same way, and compare the two groups. The first two steps decide whether the comparison is fair, and we take them one at a time, starting with the cases.
