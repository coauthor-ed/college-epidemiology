## Start from the disease, look back for the exposure

The eight women are the **cases**: people who have the disease. A design that starts from them and looks back for a cause is a **case-control study**. It needs a second group studied at the same time, the **controls**, people who do not have the disease, so that every finding about the cases has something to be set against.

<!-- STEP -->
## The two groups are chosen, not counted

How many controls? That is your decision. With one control for each woman, the study holds 16 people and half of them are ill. With four for each woman, the study holds 40 and only one in five is ill. The disease has not changed at all; only your choice has. The Boston investigators chose four controls for each case, 32 in all, girls born in the same hospital at about the same time.

**The number of people in a case-control study tells you how many were studied, never how common the disease is.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Ask both groups the same question, then name the cells

Both groups are asked the same question about the past, in the same way, and each person then falls into one of four cells: exposed cases, exposed controls, unexposed cases and unexposed controls. These cells are called a, b, c and d, and the margins a+c and b+d are simply the two groups you chose. The letters are short names for counts you can see, so read them after the people, never before.

## Three features and four steps

Three things define the design: exposure and disease have both already happened; the inquiry runs back from effect to cause; and a comparison group is required. Every case-control study then takes four steps: choose the cases, choose the controls, measure exposure in both groups in the same way, and compare. The first two steps decide whether the comparison will be fair, so we take them one at a time, starting with the cases.
