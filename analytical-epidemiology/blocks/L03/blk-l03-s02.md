## The comparison, not a risk

The answer is the last one: smoking is more common among the men with lung cancer than among the men without it. Thirty-three of the 35 patients had smoked, against 55 of the 82 controls. The table compares two groups of men, and that comparison is what it can support.

## Read down each column, not across the rows

Each column has its own group of men. The exposure rate of a group is the share of its members who were exposed, here the share who smoked. Each exposure rate divides by its own column total.
- cases: 33 of 35 smoked, which is 94.2 per cent.
- controls: 55 of 82 smoked, which is 67.0 per cent.

Tap a column header to light its numerator and its denominator together. **The rates are 94.2 and 67.0 per cent, and the gap between them is 27.2 percentage points.**

## Where the table comes from

Your textbook's Table 12 is Table V of Doll and Hill's 1950 paper. The men in the smokers row smoked fewer than five cigarettes a day, which is why the row is labelled that way. The controls were patients in the same hospitals, admitted for other diseases, not healthy men from the street. That choice of controls matters, and a later file comes back to it.

## The textbook's own summary turns the claim around

The textbook summarises this table by saying that lung cancer was higher among the smokers. The table shows something else: smoking was more common among the men who had lung cancer. One sentence claims that smokers get cancer more often, and the other says that cancer patients smoked more often. Only the second is in these counts.

**A table of patients and controls can compare exposure. It cannot say how many smokers will get cancer.**
