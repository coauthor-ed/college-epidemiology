## Could chance alone make a gap of 30 points?

*Illustrative numbers: the small study of the controls-per-case slide, with two controls per case.*

Each dot is a person: a filled dot smoked, and a hollow dot did not. Fourteen of the 20 cases smoked, an exposure rate of 70 per cent, and 16 of the 40 controls smoked, 40 per cent. The gap is 70 - 40 = 30 percentage points. With only 60 people a gap could still appear by luck, so the question is how often chance alone makes a gap this big.

<!-- STEP -->
## Shuffle the people into two new groups

A shuffle test keeps all 60 people and their smoking, and deals them at random into a group of 20 and a group of 40, the same sizes as before. It then works out the two exposure rates and their gap in the same way as for the real study. In a shuffle, being placed among the "cases" has nothing to do with smoking, so any gap it shows comes from chance alone. Each shuffled gap drops as one dot onto the number line under the people: press Shuffle once a few times to watch it.

<!-- STEP -->
## Count the shuffles that reach the real gap

After 100 shuffles most dots sit near zero, and 3 of the 100 reach the red line at 30 points. If chance alone were at work, a gap this big would turn up about 3 times in 100. That share, 0.03, is called the P value, and a small P value means chance alone is a poor explanation.

The usual cut-off is 0.05, so this gap is called statistically significant. A gap the shuffles produce easily is marked n.s., not significant, which is what the DES file's other factors were. Doll and Hill's own table has 117 men and a gap of 27.2 points. The source prints P < 0.001 for it: chance alone would make a gap that big less than once in a thousand times.

<!-- STEP -->
## What a P value cannot say

A P value answers one question: is a gap this big unlikely to come from chance? It does not say that smoking caused the cancer, and it cannot see how the men were chosen or how they were asked. **A small P value rules out luck, not a faulty comparison.** The case still has to face the question a P value cannot answer: was the comparison itself fair?
