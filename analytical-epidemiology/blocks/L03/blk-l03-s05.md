## Relative risk needs a group followed forward

The relative risk, also called the risk ratio, divides the incidence among the exposed by the incidence among the non-exposed. Incidence here means new cases in a period divided by the number of people at risk. Both incidences need a group that was followed from exposure to disease. This table has no such group: its 82 controls were chosen because they were well, not followed to see whether they stayed well.

<!-- STEP -->
## Start with the odds among the cases

An odds compares the number of men with a feature to the number without it. Among the cases, 33 men had smoked and 2 had not, so the odds of smoking among the cases are 33 to 2, or 16.5.

<!-- STEP -->
## Do the same for the controls, then divide

Among the controls, 55 had smoked and 27 had not, so their odds are 55 to 27, or 2.04. Divide one odds by the other: 16.5 / 2.04 = 8.1. The cross-product gives the same answer: (33 × 27) / (55 × 2) = 891 / 110 = 8.1. **Smoking was 8.1 times as likely among the men with lung cancer, and that number is an odds ratio.**

<!-- STEP -->
## Three conditions before the number can travel

An odds ratio estimates the relative risk fairly only when three conditions hold. The disease must be rare, the cases must represent all the cases in the population, and the controls must represent the population that produced them. The first condition decides how close 8.1 sits to a true relative risk, and the next slide tests it.
