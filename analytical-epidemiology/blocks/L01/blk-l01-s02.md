## Description ends where a hypothesis begins

Descriptive epidemiology counts cases by time, place and person, and the first thing the count gives you is the size of the problem. In 1961 the size was clear: doctors were seeing many more malformed births than before.

<!-- STEP -->
## The count also gives clues

When you look at where and when the cases fall, you find clues. In the 1961 file the clue was a new sleeping drug, thalidomide, which had been on sale in Britain since 1958.

<!-- STEP -->
## A clue next to a size becomes a hypothesis

Put the size and the clue together and you can make a supposition: thalidomide might be the cause. That supposition is a **hypothesis**, a guess drawn from observation that can be accepted or rejected. The 1961 doctors reached it informally, because they noticed the rise and then noticed the drug.

> A descriptive study ends where a hypothesis begins. An analytical study starts there and tests it.

So everything that follows depends on how well the hypothesis is written, and that is your next decision.
