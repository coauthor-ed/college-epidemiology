## Cold-case file 4: the young women with clots

The file opens on the patients, not on the drug. They are young women, most in their twenties and thirties, admitted with a clot in a deep leg vein or in the lung. Venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are rare at that age, and a clot that reaches the lungs can kill.

Between 1965 and 1969 the Committee on Safety of Drugs in Britain received 249 reports of adverse reactions in women taking oral contraceptives. Investigators therefore compared women with clots with comparable women without them: 84 cases and 168 controls, matched for age, marital status and parity (children borne).

## Turn the percentages into counts, then fill the cross-product

To use the cross-product from file 3 you need counts, and this table gives percentages. Fifty per cent of 84 cases is 42 women; 14 per cent of 168 controls is 23 women, the paper's own count (the exact share is 13.7 per cent). The scene shows the dots sorting into the four cells, 42, 23, 42 and 145, with margins of 84 and 168. Enter both cross-products yourself, 42 × 145 and 42 × 23, before you let the scene check them. ## 6.30, whatever the file calls it

Forty-two times 145 is 6090, and 42 times 23 is 966, so the ratio is 6090 / 966 = 6.30. The investigators printed it as "relative risk 6.3:1". You know this arithmetic from file 3: a case-control table computes an odds ratio, and the name printed above it does not change what the table can give. **A ratio of 6.30 built from hospital wards is only as good as the way its two groups were assembled.** Could the selection itself have manufactured part of that 6.30?
