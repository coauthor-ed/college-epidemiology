## Cold-case file 4: the young women with clots

The file opens on the patients, not on the drug. They are young women, most in their twenties and thirties, admitted with a clot in a deep leg vein or in the lung. Venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are rare at that age, and a clot that reaches the lungs can kill. Between 1965 and 1969 the Committee on Safety of Drugs in Britain received 249 reports of adverse reactions in women taking oral contraceptives, and someone had to find out whether the pill was the cause.

<!-- STEP -->
## 84 women with clots, 168 without

Investigators compared 84 women with clots with 168 comparable women without them, matched for age, marital status and parity (children borne). Half the cases, 42 women, had used oral contraceptives; of the controls, 14 per cent had, which is 23 women. Sort both groups by pill use and the four cells fill: 42, 42, 23 and 145, with margins of 84 and 168. Build the two cross-products yourself, 42 x 145 and 42 x 23, before the ratio is worked.

*Table 15: Vessey and Doll, BMJ 1969.*

## 6.30, whatever the file calls it

Forty-two times 145 is 6090, and 42 times 23 is 966, so the ratio is 6090 / 966 = 6.30. The investigators printed it as "relative risk 6.3:1". You know from the previous lesson that a case-control table computes an odds ratio, whatever name is printed above it. **A ratio of 6.30 built from hospital wards is only as good as the way its two groups were assembled.** Could the selection itself have manufactured part of it?
