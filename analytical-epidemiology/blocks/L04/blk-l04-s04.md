## The ward reads the cases more searchingly

If you chose the second answer, you read the ward correctly, because two mechanisms push in the same direction here. Recall bias: a man with a rare tumour has turned his working life over looking for a cause, so he recalls every tank-cleaning job more completely than a healthy control does. Patients who have had a myocardial infarction remember their habits better than people who have not, for the same reason. Interviewer bias: an interviewer who knows the hypothesis probes the cases' answers harder than the controls' forms, and records more exposure that way.

<!-- STEP -->
## Turn up the recall and watch the ratio rise

*Illustrative numbers.* Take a study in which the exposure truly does nothing: 30 of 60 cases and 30 of 60 controls report it, and the odds ratio is 1.00. Now let the cases' memory and the interviewer's probing add 10 percentage points to what the cases report, while nothing on the controls' side moves. Six more case men are recorded as exposed, and the ratio climbs to 1.50. The lesson is not that particular number: the whole rise came from how the two groups were asked.

## The live case's answer: the records, not the memory

Your file does not have to trust either mechanism. The counts on its table card, 14 of 20 and 9 of 60, come from the plant's personnel records, not from what anyone remembers, so recall has nothing to feed on. The remaining asymmetry is conduct. The senior's standing rule is that the person asking the questions never learns which men are the cases, and the interview records are compared afterwards to check that both groups were questioned alike. A P value, you now know, would certify none of this.
