## The door both groups walked through

Selection bias is the name for what can go wrong before any question is asked: the cases and controls a study chooses may not represent the populations they came from. Your live case chose its controls from the same workforce, so both groups entered through the same employment door. Hospital-based files, like the pill study, choose their cases and controls through the hospital's admission door, and people enter hospital at very different rates for very different conditions.

<!-- STEP -->
## Let the door build a table of its own

The scene holds a town of 2,000 people with illustrative numbers, in which the exposure and the disease have no link: 100 exposed people have the disease, and 100 unexposed people do. The town's odds ratio reads 1.00. Set the three admission chances, how readily the disease gets a person admitted, how readily the exposure does, and how readily everyone else enters for other complaints, then press "Draw the hospital sample". At the defaults, 52 exposed people with the disease, 180 exposed without it, 40 unexposed with it and 360 unexposed without it pass through the door. The hospital's own table then reads an odds ratio of 2.60. Nothing in the town has changed; the door built the association.

## A bias no amount of matching removes

This door-effect has a name, Berkson's bias, after the statistician who described it. When the exposure and the disease each raise the chance of admission, the hospital's own patients can show an association that the population behind the hospital does not have. It is worth separating it from confounding in one line. Confounding is a third feature travelling with the exposure, and matching removes it; here no third feature is at work, so no matching can undo the effect, because the selection itself created the association. A hospital file can therefore print a strong ratio that no care in the interviewing would have prevented.
