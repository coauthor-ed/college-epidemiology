## The door both groups walked through

Selection bias is the name for what can go wrong before any question is asked: the cases and controls a study chooses may not represent the populations they came from. Your live case chose its controls from the same workforce, so both groups entered through the same employment door. Hospital-based files, like the pill study, choose their cases and controls through the hospital's admission door, and people enter hospital at very different rates for very different conditions.

<!-- STEP -->
## Let the door build a table of its own

*Illustrative numbers.*

Take a town of 2,000 people in which the exposure and the disease have no link at all. The share with the disease is the same among the exposed and the unexposed, so the town's odds ratio is 1.00. Now send the town through a hospital door. The disease makes admission likely, the exposure makes it somewhat more likely, and everyone else comes in only now and then for other complaints.

Of those who pass the door, 52 are exposed with the disease, 180 exposed without it, 40 unexposed with it and 360 unexposed without it. The hospital's own table reads an odds ratio of 2.60. Nothing in the town has changed; the door built the association.

<!-- STEP -->
## A bias no amount of matching removes

This door-effect has a name, Berkson's bias, after the statistician who described it. When the exposure and the disease each raise the chance of admission, the hospital's own patients can show an association that the population behind the hospital does not have. It is worth separating it from confounding in one line. Confounding is a third feature travelling with the exposure, and matching removes it. Here no third feature is at work: the selection itself created the association, so no matching can undo it. A hospital file can therefore print a strong ratio that no care in the interviewing would have prevented.
