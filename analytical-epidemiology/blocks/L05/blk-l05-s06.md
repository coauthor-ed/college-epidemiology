## The five elements on the method card

The chapter lists the elements of a cohort study in order: selection of study subjects, obtaining data on exposure, selection of comparison groups, follow-up, and analysis. Selection comes first, and it is done in one of two ways. The cohort may come from the general population, as when the exposure is fairly common and whole communities can be assembled, as Framingham did. Or it may come from special groups that can be studied readily, such as professional groups, insured persons, or the employees of one industry, which tend to be more homogeneous and easier to reach.

<!-- STEP -->
## Four checks before anyone enters

Assembling the cohorts is gated by four conditions. The cohorts must be free of the disease under study, so if the disease is coronary heart disease the members are examined first and anyone with evidence of it is excluded. The two groups should be equally susceptible, which is why males over 35 would be an appropriate cohort for a lung cancer study. They should be comparable in respect of all the variables that can influence the frequency of the disease. And the diagnostic and eligibility criteria must be defined beforehand, because recognising the disease when it develops depends on them.

<!-- STEP -->
## Exposure and comparison, both fixed in advance

The second element is obtaining data on exposure, from records or examination, so that each member lands in the exposed or unexposed group for a stated reason. The third is the comparison group: the general population the cohort was drawn from, or another cohort thought to have had little or no exposure but otherwise similar. Both choices belong to the planning page, not to the day the results arrive.

<!-- STEP -->
## Follow-up and analysis

The groups are then followed under the same identical conditions over a period of time, and the outcome is recorded: onset of disease, disability, or death. This is the element that makes cohorts costly, because in chronic diseases such as cancer the follow-up may last decades, and it is also the element that gives the study its denominators. At the end, the incidence is worked out in both groups. If the incidence in the exposed, a/(a+b), is clearly higher than in the unexposed, c/(c+d), exposure and disease are associated. A well-designed cohort study is considered the most reliable way of showing such an association, and the exercise after the next question hands you the plant's personnel records to assemble one yourself.
