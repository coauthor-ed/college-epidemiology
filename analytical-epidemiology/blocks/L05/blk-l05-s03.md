## Two groups, nobody ill

*Illustrative numbers.* A cohort table is built out of people before it is built out of letters. One group holds 200 exposed men, the group the chapter calls the study cohort; the other holds 400 unexposed men, the control cohort. Each dot is one man. Nobody in either group has the disease, so on the first day there is nothing to count.

<!-- STEP -->
## Let the years run

Both groups are followed forward under the same conditions over the same period. As the years pass, 20 of the exposed men become cases, and 10 of the unexposed men do. The two groups were comparable people at the start, so a difference in what happens to them is exactly what this design exists to measure.

<!-- STEP -->
## Now the table can be built

With the outcomes known, the four cells of Table 16 fill. Cell a holds the 20 exposed cases and b the 180 exposed men who stayed well; cell c holds the 10 unexposed cases and d the 390 unexposed men who stayed well. The margins a+b = 200 and c+d = 400 are the people followed, the denominators that a case-control table never has.

<!-- STEP -->
## Read each row's incidence, then the ratio

The exposed row gives a/(a+b) = 20/200, an incidence of 100 per 1,000 over the follow-up. The unexposed row gives c/(c+d) = 10/400, or 25 per 1,000. Divide one by the other and the relative risk is 100 / 25 = 4.00: a formula you met in lesson 3, now a computation you have watched happen. **Incidence in each row first, then the ratio of the two incidences.**
